\begin{abstract}
Workload management is a critical operational strategy for managing pitcher performance and injury risk. Previous studies largely relied on game level data. However, with the emergence of high resolution pitch level data from Statcast, there is a need to re-evaluate the impact of fatigue. This study uses Statcast data from 2018 to 2023 to quantitatively analyze the effect of pitcher fatigue and rest days on four key pitch outcome metrics: Expected Weighted On Base Average (xwOBA), Whiff Rate, Home Run Probability, and Hard Hit Rate. Fatigue metrics are segmented into three time scales: pitches thrown in the last game (Short Term Fatigue), average pitches over the last 5 games (Medium Term Fatigue, Acute Workload Proxy), and average pitches over the last 10 games (Long Term Fatigue, Chronic Workload Proxy). Generalized Linear Models (GLM) appropriate for the data characteristics of each outcome variable will be applied to compare the time scale effects of fatigue and the efficacy of rest days. This research aims to demonstrate that long term cumulative workload holds stronger predictive power over modern pitching metrics compared to short term fatigue, suggesting that the management strategy based on the Acute Chronic Workload Ratio (ACWR) emphasized in sports science is also valid for baseball pitchers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Pitcher performance directly influences game outcomes and team standings, making pitch count management and rest day adjustments long standing, crucial operational strategies in Major League Baseball (MLB). Pitcher fatigue manifests in various forms, including decreased velocity, reduced command, and poorer batted ball quality. This is associated not only with short term performance decline but also with an increased risk of long term injuries. For these reasons, the influence of factors such as pitch count, cumulative workload, and rest days on pitcher performance has remained a critically important research topic in baseball analytics.
A seminal work among existing research, Bradbury and Forman (2012), analyzed the impact of pitcher rest days and pitch count variation on subsequent game performance using game level data. Contrary to conventional wisdom, their study concluded that a certain range of rest days (four to five days) had little effect on the next game's performance, and the influence of short term fatigue (pitch count in the immediate preceding game) was also limited. Conversely, they confirmed that long term cumulative workload was more closely associated with a decline in pitcher performance. However, this study used data from the pre Statcast era. It was therefore limited in its ability to consider modern performance indicators like contact quality and could not capture subtle pitch level changes.
Recently, advancements in Statcast and tracking data have created an environment capable of high resolution measurement of pitcher and batted ball outcomes, including pitch speed, spin rate, hard hit event occurrence, and xwOBA. Nevertheless, there remains a shortage of quantitative research that isolates fatigue by time scale—short term, medium term, and long term cumulative fatigue—and analyzes how each is reflected in actual pitch results. Furthermore, few studies have re-evaluated the effect of rest days based on pitch level data. In light of this, research is needed to re-examine and extend the conclusions of previous studies within the modern data environment.
To address this research gap, the present study utilizes Statcast based pitch level data to quantitatively analyze the impact of fatigue factors on four key pitch outcome metrics: xwOBA, whiff rate, home run probability, and hard hit event occurrence. Fatigue is measured by classifying it into pitches thrown in the immediate preceding game (short term fatigue), average pitches over the last 5 games (medium term fatigue), and average pitches over the last 10 games (long term cumulative fatigue). Furthermore, the rest day classifications used in previous research (three days, four days, and five or more days of rest) are applied identically to re-evaluate their effectiveness using modern pitch level data. The analysis will be performed using Generalized Linear Models (GLM) tailored to the data characteristics of each performance metric.
This paper is structured as follows. First, the Methods section describes the data collection process, variable construction, fatigue metric definitions, and the statistical models used for each outcome variable. The subsequent Results section presents regression outcomes for xwOBA, whiff rate, home run probability, and hard hit events, comparing the influence of fatigue metrics and rest days. The Discussion section interprets the main findings in relation to existing literature and offers practical implications for modern pitcher management strategies. Finally, the Conclusion section summarizes the findings of this research and proposes directions for future work.

\section{Methods}
# Method

This study replicates the analytical framework of **Bradbury & Forman (2012)** and **Waldrop et al. (2025)** using pitch-level Statcast data from the **2024–2025 season**. The comprehensive dataset was merged across multiple files and sorted by pitcher and game date. All analyses were conducted in **Python (pandas, statsmodels)**, and the unit of observation was either a single pitch or a pitcher-game, depending on the model specification.

---

## Fatigue Variable Construction

Pitch counts were not directly provided in the Statcast dataset, so game-level pitch counts were **reconstructed** by taking the maximum `pitch_number` for each pitcher–game combination. These values were used to compute the following pitcher-level fatigue variables:

* **Previous-game workload:** `pitch_count_last_game`
* **5-game rolling average workload:** `pitch_count_5g_avg`
* **10-game rolling average workload:** `pitch_count_10g_avg`

Days of rest were derived directly from the built-in variable **`pitcher_days_since_prev_game`**.

---

## Outcome Variables

Three pitch-level performance outcomes were modeled:

1.  **xwOBA:** `estimated_woba_using_speedangle`
2.  **Whiff (swing-and-miss):** A binary variable constructed for pitches where the `description` variable indicated a **swinging strike (`swinging_strike`)** or a **swinging strike not in play (`swinging_strike_blocked`)**.
3.  **Home run event:** An indicator for pitches resulting in a home run, based on `events` being equal to `home_run`.

For the Poisson regression model, a pitcher-game–level outcome was created:

* **Hard-hit events:** The count of pitches with **exit velocity $\ge 95$ mph**, aggregated per game.

---

## OLS Regression Models (Paper 1 Replication)

To directly replicate the structure of Bradbury & Forman (2012), **Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regressions** were fit to each pitch-level outcome: **xwOBA**, **Whiff**, and **Home run event**.

Each model included the same explanatory variables:

* `pitch_count_last_game`
* `pitch_count_5g_avg`
* `pitch_count_10g_avg`
* `days_rest`
* `arm_angle`
* `release_speed`

**Note on Binary Outcomes:** Following the methodology of the original study (Bradbury & Forman, 2012), OLS was employed even for the binary outcomes (**Whiff** and **Home run event**) to ensure **direct and comparable replication**. As pitch-level outcomes are highly stochastic, low *R*-squared values were expected, and interpretation focused primarily on **coefficient direction** and **statistical significance** rather than predictive power.

---

## Poisson Regression Model (Paper 2 Replication)

To mirror the approach of Waldrop et al. (2025), a **Poisson regression** was estimated at the pitcher-game level with **hard-hit count** as the dependent variable. The **total pitches per game** was included as a **log offset** to model the *rate* of hard-hit contact rather than the raw count.

The predictors were:

* **`rest_grp`** (A binary variable distinguishing between $\ge 5$ days versus $< 5$ days of rest.)
* `pitch_count_last_game`
* `pitch_count_10g_avg`

**Incidence Rate Ratios (IRRs)** were calculated by exponentiating the model coefficients ($IRR = e^{\beta}$), allowing interpretation of how rest patterns and long-term fatigue influence the **rate** of hard-hit contact per pitch thrown.
